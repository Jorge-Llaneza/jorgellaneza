What the game does to you

  1. Three landmarks first. Middle C, the G line and the C above it (treble); middle C, the F line and bass C (bass). Every new note is shown as a distance from one of them: D is a step above middle C, E a skip below the G line.
  2. New notes come in small groups, once every note in play has come out right and fast three times, spread over at least a minute (under 2.5 s on a piano, 2 s on the computer keyboard, 3 s through the mic). The first time a note shows up its name is printed under it, so that answer doesn't count.
  3. Misses get named and fixed on the spot. A step off means line and space got mixed up; a skip off means you counted one line or space too many. You play the right key, and the note comes back twice: just after the notes on screen, and again about a dozen notes later.
  4. The bar rises. To count as fluent a note has to come out in about half the starting time, and stay that fast on later days: the top three levels are a day or more apart, and a miss drops a note two levels. Hover a note in the map to see its level.
  5. Notes you know show up less. Shaky and slow ones show up more. Ones you haven't seen in a day or more get pulled back in, so long gaps don't wipe what you learned.
  6. You always see the next four notes. In melodic order they move mostly by step and skip, so you read each note as a distance from the last one. With a pulse they move on with the click whether you played or not: your eyes have to stay ahead, and you don't stop to fix mistakes.
  7. Every answer sounds. The dot, the key and the pitch get stored together.
  8. Short sessions. One 60-second sprint a day beats a single hour on Sunday.
