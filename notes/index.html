What the game does to you

  1. Three landmarks first. Middle C, the G line and the C above it (treble); middle C, the F line and bass C (bass). Every other note gets read relative to these.
  2. New notes come in small groups, only when every note in play has been answered right and fast three times running (under 2 s on the computer keyboard, 2.5 s on a piano, 3 s through the mic). The first time a note shows up, its name is printed under it. After that you're on your own.
  3. Misses get fixed on the spot. You see the right name and key, play it, and the same note comes back three notes later.
  4. Notes you know show up less. Shaky and slow ones show up more. Ones you haven't seen in a day or more get pulled back in, so long gaps don't wipe what you learned.
  5. You always see the next four notes. Your eyes learn to sit one note ahead of your hands, which is what real reading needs.
  6. Every answer sounds. The dot, the key and the pitch get stored together.
  7. Short sessions. One 60-second sprint a day beats a single hour on Sunday.
